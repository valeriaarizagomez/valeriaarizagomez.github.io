My research examines how news media and political discourse construct minoritized groups, negotiate symbolic boundaries, and shape conditions of visibility, voice, and belonging. My current projects combine computational text analysis and critical discourse approaches to compare representations across countries and media types.

Patterns and Contrasts: Two Decades of Minoritized and Dominant-Group Representation in U.S. Broadsheet News

Work in progress

A longitudinal analysis of how U.S. broadsheets represented LGBTQ+ people, migrants, disabled people, and religious minorities between 2002 and 2021, using unmarked men as a dominant-group reference point. The study compares salience, stance, sentiment, and topic distributions to examine patterns of representational inequality.

Read abstract

Minoritized groups’ representation in news media is closely linked to public attitudes and behaviors toward them, shaping their social meaning and broader dynamics of exclusion and inclusion. This study examines U.S. broadsheet news representations of LGBTQ+ people, migrants, disabled people, and religious minorities between 2002 and 2021, alongside unmarked men as a dominant-group reference point. Unmarked men provide a benchmark for situating minoritized groups’ representation within media discourse and identifying whether observed patterns are group-specific, common across coverage of minoritized groups, or consistent with broader news practices.

Using computational text analysis, we trace changes in salience, stance, sentiment, and topic distribution across coverage of these groups. The study makes two empirical contributions. First, it traces each group’s positioning in broadsheet news over two decades. Second, by using unmarked men as a comparison point, it examines patterns in dominant-group representation, an angle that remains under-researched.

Results show that coverage of minoritized groups increased and diversified, but consistently positioned them within established stereotypical frames. Coverage was predominantly oppositional, sentiment was mainly negative, and articles concentrated on a narrow set of news topics. Migrants, religious minorities, and LGBTQ+ people were persistently framed alongside crime, legality, morality, and politics, whereas disabled people were represented more variably. Coverage of unmarked men was also largely oppositional toward them, but was distributed across a wider range of topics throughout the two decades. Implications for representational inequality are discussed.

The Shifting Boundaries of Inclusion: A Comparative Analysis of Minoritized Group Portrayals in German and Polish Media

Work in progress

A comparative study of how German and Polish broadsheets and tabloids portrayed migrants, LGBTQ+ people, and disabled people between 2012 and 2024. Drawing on representation theory and symbolic boundaries, the paper examines how visibility, stance, and thematic positioning vary across political contexts, time, and newspaper types.

Read abstract

Across Europe, political contestation over migration, gender, sexuality, and disability has placed minoritized groups at the center of public debate. Recent scholarship has identified systematic boundary-drawing and othering of minoritized groups in far-right parties’ discourse and far-right alternative media. Less is known, however, about how broadsheet and tabloid newspapers, given their broad reach and agenda-setting power, reproduce, amplify, or challenge such exclusionary discourse.

Drawing on Stuart Hall’s theory of representation and Lamont and Molnár’s concept of symbolic boundaries, this paper examines how German and Polish mainstream newspapers constructed migrants, LGBTQ+ people, and disabled people between 2012 and 2024. The comparison captures contrasting political trajectories: the electoral consolidation of the Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) in Germany and Poland’s shift from the illiberal rule of the Law and Justice party (PiS) toward re-liberalization. Using salience analysis, stance analysis, and seeded topic modelling, the study analyzes coverage across political contexts, time, and media types.

Results show that all three groups became more visible over time in both countries, but without a corresponding increase in their centrality to coverage. In Germany, broadsheets generally situated minoritized groups within political and social debates, whereas tabloids more often foregrounded individual experiences and confined groups to contentious frames such as crime and morality. In Poland, broadsheets covered minoritized groups most often in articles about crime, politics, human rights, and social justice. Polish tabloids most often portrayed the groups in articles about health and safety, sports, and crime.

The findings position mainstream newspapers as contested discursive arenas in which social differences are defined, amplified, or challenged. They suggest that exclusionary coverage contributes to symbolic boundaries that political actors may mobilize. By combining cross-national, longitudinal, and media-type comparisons, the study contributes to research on media representation, illiberalism, and the mediated construction of minoritized groups.

Voices at the Intersection: Migrant Identities in the Press and Parliaments of the UK and Germany

Work in progress

A cross-national study of how parliamentary debates and broadsheet news represent migrant identities at the intersections of race, gender, disability, and religion. Combining computational text analysis and critical discourse analysis, the project examines voice, agency, belonging, and the circulation of representative claims between political and media arenas from 2011 to 2021.

Read abstract

Intersectional migrant identities constitute small, often marginalized groups whose visibility and agency in the public sphere remain poorly understood. Although migrants have become increasingly salient in policy debates and mainstream news, this attention rarely extends to those at the intersection of migration and race, gender, disability, or religion. Grounded in boundary-making theory and an interactional understanding of intersectionality, this article treats representations of intersectional migrant identities as discursive mechanisms through which their belonging and public standing are constructed and contested.

We analyze parliamentary speeches in the UK and Germany alongside broadsheet news coverage in both countries between 2011 and 2021 as arenas in which representative claims about intersectional migrant groups are produced and circulated. We ask: (1) Under which conditions do political and media actors give voice to, or take voice from, intersectional migrant groups? (2) Do representative claims about them travel between parliamentary debates and broadsheet news?

Using computational text analysis, we map the salience of migrant identities in speeches and news articles against key political events. We then conduct critical discourse analysis on a purposive sample to examine how linguistic strategies construct intersectional migrant groups as political subjects. We analyze who speaks to, for, and about them; whether they appear as quoted sources or subjects; and how agency, responsibility, and belonging are discursively attributed to them.

The study is designed to provide a cross-national mapping of representations of intersectional migrant identities in two arenas of the public sphere over a decade of contentious migration politics. It examines which intersectional configurations receive attention, under which conditions, and whether specific representative claims travel between parliamentary debates and news coverage. By comparing two European democracies with distinct institutional and media systems, the project investigates how public debates on migration shape the supply side of representative claims about intersectional migrant groups.
